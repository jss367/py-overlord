Two-player strategy search · September 19, 2026

Collection and Swindler

The best policy found is a Collection scoring rush with two Swindlers, and no planned Imperial Envoy purchases. Open with attacks, accumulate Collections, then buy repeated cheap Actions for victory tokens. Start taking Provinces from your twelfth turn.

The winner scored 68.8% in the 15-policy round robin. The initial study used 75,360 games, including 16,000 independent validation games. This is the strongest tested policy, not a proof of optimal play.

Read the dedicated Village–Imperial Envoy follow-up, including larger engines, selective drawing and fresh validation.

Follow-up: a dedicated Village and Imperial Envoy challenge

The initial search did not establish the strongest possible engine. Its Envoy policies used fixed acquisition priorities, and would play Envoy even with no cards left to draw. That still grants a Buy but takes two debt. The user's challenge prompted a separate engine study rather than treating the original ranking as settled.

The follow-up ran 92,000 additional games: 408 engine configurations in the first screen; 270 configurations in refinement (including Gold- and Mystic-income alternatives); 96 variants of the earlier policy with selective draw; 18 confirmed finalists; and 14,000 fresh validation games. The screen used three fixed random candidate-generation seeds. Budgets and all resulting configurations are recorded in the evidence below.

The published rush remains the recommendation among tested policies. The strongest confirmed challenger used one Envoy and scored 42.3% against the rush on 2,000 fresh games (approximate 95% interval 40.6%–44.0%). The strongest confirmed challenger targeting multiple Envoys scored 31.9% (30.1%–33.7%), also over 2,000 fresh games.

What the stronger engine search changed

  • Interleaved draw and income purchases instead of buying all Envoys before Collections; varied income-first, draw-first and balanced orders.
  • Tested targets up to six Envoys, eight Villages and eight Collections, with Ghost Town support, zero to two Swindlers after the opening, early and delayed Provinces, and different token-purchase timing.
  • Skipped Envoy below a tuned remaining-card threshold, used spare Actions for attacks before drawing, and preserved the last Action for Envoy when it could find more villages.
  • Tested $5 openings on Envoy or Collection, alternate $3/$4 openings, Gold as core income, and Mystic support.
  • Kept the original acquisition family as a separate challenger with improved draw decisions. Neither the published rush nor the underlying card rules changed during this follow-up.

The two frozen finalists

The closest challenger targets one Envoy, six Villages, four Collections and two Swindlers, with no Ghost Town purchases. It delays Provinces until only two remain (the implementation's turn-99 fallback is effectively beyond the tested games), and buys scoring Actions with at least one Collection played. It follows the original acquisition order, so these targets are not an instruction to buy all six Villages immediately.

The multiple-Envoy finalist targets two Envoys, six Villages, six Collections, two Ghost Towns and two Swindlers, with the same delayed Province and token-purchase rules. In its held-out rush matchup, it actually ended with 1.97 Envoys, 3.54 Villages and 3.98 Collections on average, and played Envoy 6.7 times per game. Mean score was 19.7 against the rush's 27.9.

How to interpret the extra buys

Village and Envoy do form a draw engine, and Envoy's extra Buy is useful. The rush already gets additional Buys from Collection and Fishmonger. My interpretation of these results is that draw must pay for its own construction and recurring debt before those extra Buys improve scoring. A drawn Collection provides $2 while each Envoy adds two debt; Swindler also attacks the expensive pieces. Those interactions explain why an engine is plausible, but they are not a proof that the rush must win.

How to play it

  1. On a $3/$4 opening, buy Swindler/Swindler. The tested $5/$2 opening also buys Swindler, with Fishmonger on $2. That unusual $5 opening was not separately optimized; do not interpret it as proof that passing a first-turn Collection is best.
  2. Take Collection at your first later $5, and build toward four owned copies. Replace Swindlers lost to attacks until you own two again. Counts include replacement cards gained from opponents' Swindlers.
  3. Buy up to two Villages as ordinary support. When no earlier target is affordable, Gold is a fallback up to two owned copies, then Silver up to two, then your first Fishmonger.
  4. Whenever at least one Collection has been played, keep buying Actions for tokens. After the earlier deck targets, use this order: Village, Fishmonger, Sleigh, Swindler, Tent. These token purchases have no copy cap. A second or third Village bought this way can be more valuable than adding Silver.
  5. From your turn 12, Province takes priority when affordable. Also allow Provinces whenever only two remain. Buy Duchy with three or fewer Provinces left and Estate with one or fewer. Track scores and the empty piles: cheap Action piles and Curses can end the game before Provinces run out.
  6. Play Village before terminals, then Swindler before Fishmonger. Use spare Actions for the Fishmongers available through Shadow. Play all Collections before making purchases. The winning policy does not deliberately rotate the Forts or buy Ghost Town, Imperial Envoy, Merchant Ship or Mystic.

These are priorities, not a fixed final deck recipe. Across validation, the winner ended with about 3.8 Collections, 5.5 Villages, 5.1 Fishmongers, 2.1 Swindlers and 2.3 Provinces. Average score: 37.1, including 15.7 victory tokens.

Why this plan works

Collection turns purchases into scoring. Each copy played grants a token for each Action gained that turn. With three Collections played, two Village purchases earn six tokens while improving the deck. Collection also supplies money and another Buy. See the official Prosperity rules.

Swindler pressures an unthinned deck. There is no voluntary trasher in this kingdom. Copper can become Curse; $5 cards can become Duchy. The search gives opponents damaging replacements rather than using ordinary shopping priorities. The official Intrigue rules require the attacker to choose a same-cost replacement.

More draw did not automatically help. Imperial Envoy draws five but adds two debt each time it is played, reducing the money left for token purchases. Several Envoy/Ghost Town variants were competitive, but the no-Envoy policy won the final tournament. The results compare implemented plans; they do not establish that every possible Envoy engine loses.

Ghost Town and Sleigh have timing limits. Ghost Town is Night–Duration, not an Action, so gaining it earns no Collection tokens. Sleigh's Horses are normally gained during the Action phase, before Collections have been played. They therefore do not automatically turn Sleigh into an extra Collection scoring source.

Tent can expose later Forts, but the winning policy skips deliberate rotation. Only the exposed card can be gained from a split pile; see the official Allies rules.

Reproduce the search

Run from the repository root. The game seeds are fixed and seats alternate. Random candidate generation uses two fixed seeds. This is a bounded parameter search, not an exhaustive search of legal strategies. It uses fixed tactical priorities and the simulator's existing purchase-ending guard; it does not solve every multi-buy ending.
