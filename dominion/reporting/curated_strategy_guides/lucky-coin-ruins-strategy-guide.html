Lucky Coin and Ruins:
race with Tea Houses

The recommended policy opens Tea House at $5, or Banquet for Tea House at $3–$4, adds one Highwayman and one Mining Road, and uses Kind Emperor to gain Daimyos without debt. Start buying Provinces early. A direct 10,000-game comparison against the retuned Shrine/Spice Merchant build favored this plan, 55.07% to 44.93%.

The recommendation is a strategy family, not a proven mathematical optimum.

Banquet and direct-purchase openings lead to the same core deck. Initial validation found a small advantage for buying Tea House directly at $5. That improvement was selected before a second independent validation run.

Decision-quality follow-up: Saved-position audits and shared lookahead player now include focused cleanup searches, independently confirmed Shrine and Spice Merchant choices, and fresh full-game comparisons. These tests do not establish optimal human play.

How to play it

1. Get income moving

On each of your first two turns, buy Tea House directly at $5. With $3–$4, use Banquet to gain Tea House and two Coppers. Prefer this immediate income to a slow cleanup project. Pass if you cannot afford a useful purchase.

2. Add draw and Buys

After two Tea Houses, add one Highwayman and one Mining Road. Build toward five Tea Houses. Lucky Coin supplies Silvers; Mining Road can immediately play a gained Silver. Highwayman provides three cards next turn.

3. Gain free multipliers

When Kind Emperor activates, use its immediate gain and later start-of-turn gains to finish missing components and take Daimyos. Play them before Tea House. Buy Provinces whenever $8 is available once you have a Tea House.

Exact purchase and free-gain priorities

After the opening, prioritize Province; add Duchy at three or fewer Provinces and Estate at one or fewer. For development, first reach two Tea Houses, then one Highwayman, then one Mining Road, then five Tea Houses. When a desired $5 card is unaffordable, Banquet can gain it for $3 while adding two Coppers. The policy stops using Banquet once it owns five Tea Houses.

Kind Emperor gains to your hand: finish two Tea Houses → get Highwayman → get Mining Road → reach two Daimyos → reach five Tea Houses. Once those targets are met, keep taking Daimyos while available. If a pile is gone, move to the next available target. The runnable source records the remaining fallbacks.

Stack Daimyos before the next non-Command Action: two Daimyos make a Tea House play three times. With this build, Tea House is the preferred target; Mining Road is the next useful target when Tea House is absent. Mining Road replays supply additional Buys, coins, and opportunities to play gained Treasures. Kind Emperor's free Daimyo gains never incur its purchase debt. See the publisher's Rising Sun rules, pages 5 and 9.

Under Highwayman, play a Copper first to absorb the first-Treasure block, then Lucky Coin. Otherwise play Lucky Coin first. Multiple Highwayman attacks still suppress only the first Treasure; additional copies primarily supply more duration draw. Mining Road plays the Treasure just gained; it does not gain a Gold or another Treasure. Each play supplies a separate allowance, and the same gained Treasure cannot use two allowances. See the Plunder rules, pages 4–6.

What about the other plans?

  • Spice Merchant + Lost City: the closest distinct competitor. Open Spice Merchant when affordable, otherwise use Banquet for Tea House. Build two Tea Houses, one Lost City, one Spice Merchant, one or two Highwaymen, one Mining Road, and free Daimyos. Trash Coppers for two cards and an Action. This family narrowly led the ten-way tournament; the faster Banquet build led the later comparison of the top four policies.
  • Secluded Shrine + Lucky Coin: a real synergy. Play Shrine, leave unwanted Ruins in hand, then play Lucky Coin to trigger up to two trashes. The original policy had important limitations; the dedicated cleanup audit below tests stronger decisions. Its results still favor the Tea House recommendation, without establishing optimal Shrine play.
  • Battle Plan into Warlord: rotate Clashes twice to expose Warlord. The dedicated search included one or two Warlords, with and without Shrine, Lost City, and Highwayman. It trailed the leading economy builds. If your opponent plays Warlord, remember that it limits physical copies played from hand; Daimyo replays still work. Two Tea Houses can therefore produce more than two plays. See the Allies rules, pages 8–9.
  • Change: Ruins can become Tea Houses for five debt or Provinces for eight debt; a subsequent Change play while debt remains produces $3. These upgrades are legal, but the tested Change builds lost too much development time. Those Change-specific results also predate the mandatory-gain correction described in the lookahead audit; rerun them under the strict rules before using them as current strength evidence. Do not infer that every possible Change policy is inferior.
  • Scrounge: it can trash any card from hand. Its other mode retrieves an Estate already in the trash and then gains a card costing up to $5. There are no initial Estates here, so that mode needs setup. It cannot trash an Estate from the Supply. See the Plunder rules, page 11.
  • Fool or purchased Daimyo openings: neither led the opening screen. Fool also supplies Lucky Coin at setup without requiring you to buy Fool.

Follow-up: was cleanup played well enough?

The original search did not establish optimal Shrine play. Its player could choose Highwayman over Shrine on its last Action, spend Ruins that could instead be trashed through Banquet's Copper gains, and fail to buy a Treasure specifically to activate an armed Shrine. Those limitations made the initial dismissal of cleanup too strong.

The follow-up preserved the original policy and added a separate cleanup player. It reserves Ruins while Shrine is armed, considers useful Shrine plays before other terminals, can buy Copper or Silver to trigger trashing, and can preserve a $3/$5/$8 buying threshold instead of trashing every available Copper. Variants also tested doubling Shrine, one or two Shrines, one or two Highwaymen, more or less Lost City draw, combined Spice Merchant cleanup, avoiding Banquet, Spice Merchant's money mode and Silver fuel, and gaining Shrine free when Lucky Coin and at least two Ruins are in hand.

There were 62,560 additional games: 22,560 screening games for 94 candidates, 16,000 refinement games for 50 settings, and 24,000 fresh validation games. Four candidates were selected before validation. Each faced the frozen Tea House recommendation, the original strong two-Highwayman Spice Merchant engine, and the original decision policy with its own deck targets, for 2,000 games per matchup. No game hit the turn cap.

Game trace audit: saving Copper for Shrine

The previous cleanup player still played every Copper before buying. Replaying the first 400 games of its earlier validation (seeds 5,000,000–5,000,199, both seats) found zero turns with Copper carried into the Buy phase. A conservative detector found 17 missed opportunities in 17 games: Shrine left a trash unused, an income-producing Copper had already been played, and withholding its $1 would have preserved affordability at every actual purchase.

What changed

When Shrine is armed and only Coppers remain to be played, the new policy compares playing them all with withholding up to the available trash capacity. It simulates only the remaining Copper plays and the current Buy phase, preserving all non-Copper purchases, debt payments, and Ruins cleanup. It holds Copper when that produces a lower ending Copper count. Random state is restored and no future-turn or hidden-draw outcome is scored.

It also stops treating an unplayed Copper as spendable income once purchases have begun: on this board, that Copper can no longer be played normally, so trashing it cannot reduce buying power. A separate ordering option lets Mining Road play a newly gained Silver before Shrine chooses its trashes, making that income available to the purchase-threshold calculation.

In a 400-game replay with both corrections, Copper was carried into the Buy phase on 113 turns and the conservative missed-opportunity count fell to zero. These are diagnostic replays, not extra independent validation games. Observational trace tests confirm that recording decisions leaves the previous policy's game outcomes unchanged.

Evidence and limits

The initial investigation contained 146,540 games: 82,540 screening and refinement games, an 18,000-game ten-policy tournament, a 6,000-game comparison of four leaders, 20,000 initial validation games that identified the opening improvement, and 20,000 final validation games. No recorded game hit the turn cap.

The search varied openings, engine size, trashing, attack counts, free gains, multiplier targets, and Province timing. It included clean Tea House builds before adding support, then refined interacting settings together. Purchase decisions and Banquet/Scrounge free gains use separate selectors; Kind Emperor has its own hand-aware selector. Finalists retained the best Spice Merchant, Shrine, Change, and Warlord families. The first independent validation favored a direct $5 Tea House purchase over always buying Banquet. The revised recommendation was selected before the second independent validation seeds were run.

Before collecting results, the simulator was corrected for Scrounge's two modes, Mining Road's gained-Treasure play and cumulative allowances, Banquet's choice and exposed-pile legality, and Fool's transfer of the unique Lost in the Woods state. Regression tests cover these interactions and the exact custom start.

Limits: this searches parameterized heuristic policies, not the complete space of legal play. It does not optimize every opening-hand decision, every Boon order, arbitrary debt repayment timing, or every tactical pile ending. Fool uses the simulator's existing Boon resolution order. Different player counts, starting-card order, Colonies, or additional scenario rules can change the recommendation.
