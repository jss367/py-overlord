A kingdom of unused cards · September 17, 2026

Ten cards nobody played.
Four of them carry the board.

The kingdom is the ten strongest piles in the catalog that no runnable strategy referenced. The search found a Scrying Pool engine that wins 79% of held-out games — and found that three of the ten piles do nothing for it, and three more are traps.

The kingdom

Ambassador$3 · Action–Attack
Sea Hag$4 · Action–Attack
Fortress$4 · Action
Governor$5 · Action
Minion$5 · Action–Attack
Old Witch$5 · Action–Attack
Ill-Gotten Gains$5 · Treasure
Bridge Troll$5 · Action–Attack–Duration
Goons$6 · Action–Attack
Scrying Pool$2 + Potion · Action–Attack

Two players, the normal basic supply, seven Coppers and three Estates each. No Shelters, Colonies, Platinum, or landscapes. Scrying Pool costs a Potion, so the Alchemy Potion joins the supply; nothing else is added.

These ten were chosen as the strongest piles with zero strategy references in the catalog, not as a balanced kingdom, and the result shows it. Three separate cursers (Sea Hag, Old Witch, Ill-Gotten Gains) chase a ten-card Curse pile. There is exactly one village, Fortress. There is one trasher, Ambassador, and it is also an attack. The only non-terminal draw is Scrying Pool, behind a Potion, and Governor, which hands the opponent a share of everything it does.

The tension. Every obvious plan on this board is a money plan: three cursers and a Goons make a perfectly good Big Money deck, and that deck is what the engines have to beat. The board's only real engine runs on a $2-plus-Potion card that does nothing until the deck is almost all Actions.

The recommendation: Scrying Pool, Fortress and Goons

Open Potion. Buy one Ambassador and use it on Estates and Curses only. Fill the deck with Scrying Pools over Fortresses until it draws itself, then add Goons and spend every buy. This policy won a fourteen-entrant round robin at 71.6% over 1,040 games, then scored 79.0% across 3,000 further games on fresh shuffle seeds against ten opponents, beating every one of them.

Open the runnable strategy.

Opening and buying

  1. Potion first, on turn 1 or 2. Across 60 traced games the Potion arrived on turn 1.6 and the first Ambassador on turn 2.6. One Potion is enough and a second is worse: the refine sweep scored three Potions at 0.403 against one Potion at 0.680.
  2. One Ambassador, not three. It returns Estates and Curses. Removing it entirely costs the deck 90.8% of a 300-game head-to-head, so the single copy is load-bearing — but it is a thinner, not a plan.
  3. Keep three Coppers. The economy floor is the difference between a thin deck and a broke one. Without it the Ambassador deck shrinks to six or seven cards, cannot reach $5, and scores nothing; that failure is reproduced below.
  4. Scrying Pools over Fortresses, eight to five. Scrying Pool reveals the run of Actions on top of the deck and puts all of them in hand, so every Action added makes every Pool draw further. Fortress is the only village and never leaves the deck.
  5. Goons late, then buy everything. Goons first appears around turn 22 in traced games. Each one in play turns every buy into a victory token, and the deck spends its spare buys on Estates and then Coppers.
  6. Green only when four Provinces are gone. There is no turn-count trigger. Greening on a turn counter instead scored 0.45 or worse on every setting tried, against 0.573 for the Province-count rule.

Playing the deck

Fortress first, then Scrying Pool, then the terminals. Scrying Pool's reveal is used as a filter: on your own deck it discards anything that is not an Action, because a Copper on top both misses the draw and stops it. On the opponent's deck it does the reverse and discards their best card. Setting that policy to “discard only junk” instead cost 0.448 against 0.573.

Ambassador is never played with nothing to return. Revealing a card the deck intends to keep hands the opponent a free copy of it, so a hand with no Estate, Curse or spare Copper simply plays something else.

What the winning deck looks like

Averaged over the validation games it finished with about 5.3 Scrying Pools, 5.1 Fortresses, 3.9 Goons, 2.3 Bridge Trolls, 1.9 Sea Hags, one Old Witch, one Ambassador, one Potion and one Silver, alongside 22.5 Coppers and 9.2 Estates bought as Goons fodder. It buys no Gold at all. Games ran about 27 to 45 turns depending on the opponent, and 2,523 of the 3,000 validation games ended on three empty piles rather than on an emptied Province pile.

Four piles carry this board. Scrying Pool, Fortress, Ambassador and Goons are each worth a decisive margin. The three cursers and the Bridge Troll in the published deck are coin flips at 300 games: the winning parameters buy them, and removing them changes nothing measurable. They are in the published strategy because that is the deck the 3,000 validation games measured, not because they were shown to help.

Three of the ten are traps. Governor is the clearest: every setting of it scored worse than none, from 0.445 for a single copy down to 0.263 for three. Its draw, its Gold, and its upgrade all give the opponent a weaker copy of the same thing, and against a money deck that gift is worth more to them than the card is to you. Ill-Gotten Gains rushes were the weakest archetype in the round robin at 4.1%, and Minion never beat the leader on any sweep. All three were rejected by the search, not omitted from it, and all three are still listed as unreferenced in the card usage report after this work.

The money plan, and why it loses

The board's natural money deck is Sea Hag plus Old Witch to empty the Curse pile, two Goons for points per buy, and otherwise Silver, Gold and immediate Provinces. It is registered as a runnable strategy because it is the yardstick the engine had to clear, and because it was the best thing found before the engine was reachable at all.

It is genuinely good against the obvious alternatives. It beats plain Old Witch money, it beats Big Money, and it beat every hand-built engine tried early in this work. Against the published engine it wins 2.0% of 300 games.

The reason is buys. A money deck converts $8 into one Province; the Scrying Pool deck draws most of its cards and converts one turn into a Province plus five or six scored buys under Goons. The money deck's final scores cluster around 42 points and the engine's around 122.

Simulator work done for this board

Every pile here was unused, which means none of them had been exercised by a strategy. A card-by-card audit against the printed rules found five behaviour defects and four decisions the strategy layer could not reach. All are fixed, with tests in tests/test_jerusalem_board_cards.py.

CardDefectEffect
Bridge TrollThe attack handed out the −1 Card token. The card gives the −$1 token.Wrong attack entirely: victims lost a card from their next hand instead of a coin.
Bridge Troll“Cards cost $1 less on your turn” was applied on every player's turn.A Duration Troll stays in play through the opponent's turn, so it was discounting their buys too — a gift to the player it attacks.
Ill-Gotten GainsGranted +1 Buy it does not have.Straight buff to every Ill-Gotten Gains rush, the archetype most likely to exploit it.
GovernorThe upgrade gained a card costing up to $2 more instead of exactly $2 more.Turned an exact-cost trade into a free remodel.
GovernorEach other player upgraded by up to $2 instead of exactly $1.Opponents got the player's own version of the effect rather than the weaker one, roughly doubling what Governor gives away.
AmbassadorHow many copies to return was hardcoded by card name.No strategy could return two of a non-junk card, or return zero and hand an opponent a copy. It is now a decision, with the old behaviour as the default.

Four per-card decisions had a hook on the base AI that GeneticAI never forwarded to the strategy, so no strategy could override them: choose_governor_option, choose_card_to_ambassador, choose_ambassador_return_count, and choose_topdeck_or_discard (Scrying Pool's and Spy's reveal). All four are now forwarded. Strategies that define no hook keep the previous defaults unchanged.

How the search ran, and where it went wrong first

The pipeline is a broad screen, then one-field sweeps, then combinations of the sweeps' winners, then a round robin, then held-out validation. Every stage plays each shuffle seed from both starting seats.

StageGamesResult

Three things that nearly buried the answer

Each of these made the winning plan look worthless, and each is a search-harness bug rather than a fact about Dominion. They are recorded because the failure modes are general.

  1. A default that added cards nobody asked for. The parameter defaults carried three Governors, so every plan written as “defaults plus these cards” silently contained the one card that ruins this board. The Scrying Pool panel entry scored 0.150 against plain money with them and 0.700 without. Kingdom counts now default to zero, so a plan contains exactly the piles it names.
  2. Knobs that did nothing. Two acquisition orders listed Silver and Gold before the kingdom cards. With unlimited Silver and Gold targets, nothing after them was ever bought, so four parameters read as tuned while having no effect — four probes returned rates identical to the baseline. Silver and Gold now close every order.
  3. A thinner with no floor. Ambassador returned Coppers without limit, so the deck thinned itself from twelve cards to six, could no longer reach $5, and finished games with 0 points and no treasure at all. The floor keeps three Coppers, minus one for each bought Treasure that has replaced them.

The final combination stage also overfits its panel, and that is visible in the record: the combination ranked first against the panel at 0.944 is the published winner, but the runner-up at 0.938 loses to it head-to-head. The round robin and the held-out validation exist to catch exactly that, and the published choice is the one that survived both.

These are simulator comparisons, not a proof of optimal Dominion play. Opponent selection, the policies' tactical defaults, and finite samples limit the conclusion. The three neutral piles above are neutral at 300 games; a larger sample could separate them.

Reproduce the experiments

Run these from the repository root, in order. Each stage reads the previous stage's record.

PYTHONPATH=. python scripts/search_jerusalem.py search \
    --games 40 --output .context/jerusalem-search.json

PYTHONPATH=. python scripts/search_jerusalem.py refine \
    --input .context/jerusalem-search.json \
    --games 40 --finalists 3 --output .context/jerusalem-refine.json

PYTHONPATH=. python scripts/search_jerusalem.py combine \
    --input .context/jerusalem-search.json \
    --refine-input .context/jerusalem-refine.json \
    --games 40 --output .context/jerusalem-combine.json

PYTHONPATH=. python scripts/search_jerusalem.py tournament \
    --input .context/jerusalem-combine.json \
    --games 80 --finalists 10 --output .context/jerusalem-tournament.json

PYTHONPATH=. python scripts/search_jerusalem.py validate \
    --input .context/jerusalem-combine.json \
    --games 300 --output .context/jerusalem-validation.json

PYTHONPATH=. python scripts/search_jerusalem.py ablate \
    --input .context/jerusalem-combine.json \
    --games 300 --output .context/jerusalem-ablation.json

To play the published strategy against any other registered entrant on this board:

python compare_all_strategies.py --board boards/jerusalem.txt --games 40

Because every pile here was unused, most registered strategies reference a card this board does not have and the tool excludes them. The substantive comparisons are the round robin and the validation above.
