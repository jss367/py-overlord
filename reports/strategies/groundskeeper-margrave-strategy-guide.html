Ten unused piles.
One of them is a trap.

Two players, the normal basic supply, seven Coppers and three Estates each. There are no Shelters, Colonies, Platinum, Potion, or landscapes of any kind. The board is deliberately plain so the comparison is about the ten piles and nothing else.

The cost curve covers $2 through $6 and the roles are paired: two villages (Fishing Village, Border Village), two draw terminals (Margrave, Library), two trashers (Moneylender, Junk Dealer), two sifters (Cellar, Oasis), and two competing payloads — Monument and Groundskeeper. That last pair is the board's actual question.

The tension. Monument is a terminal that gives +$2 and one victory-point token every time it is played. Groundskeeper gives +1 Card, +1 Action and then one token for every victory card gained for the rest of the turn. Monument pays a flat rate and helps you buy; Groundskeeper pays a multiple but only if you are already gaining several green cards a turn.

The recommendation: Margrave, Library and Monument

Build a Fishing Village engine with two Libraries, one Margrave and two Junk Dealers, and take the points from two Monuments. Buy no Groundskeeper at all. This policy won the twelve-entrant finalist round robin at 74.8% with ties counted as half a win, then scored 76.3% across 2,100 further games on fresh shuffle seeds against seven opponents, beating every one of them.

Opening and buying

  1. Open Silver, then the best engine piece you can afford. Across 60 traced games the deck bought a Silver on turn 1 in 59 of them, then on turn 2 took a Fishing Village at $3, a Monument at $4, or a Margrave at $5 depending on the split.
  2. Skip Moneylender, but not because it is unplayable. The winner buys none, yet the second- and third-place finalists each ran one and finished 3.1 and 6.3 points behind it. This is the least settled choice in the list.
  3. Buy Fishing Villages freely up to four. They are $3, they are never dead, and their duration half means the next turn starts with +1 Action and +$1 already banked. In traced games the first arrives around turn 3.
  4. Add the payload early and the draw late. Monument lands around turn 4 and Margrave around turn 5, before the first Junk Dealer (turn 6) and well before the first Library (turn 7.5). Library is held back deliberately until the villages can support it; Border Village arrives last, around turn 10.
  5. Cap the draw at two Libraries and one Margrave. The third draw terminal loses to a Silver; Margrave's +1 Buy is what earns its single slot, not its cards.
  6. Green from turn 12, or as soon as four Provinces are gone. The traced first Province came on turn 12.3, the first Duchy on turn 15.1, the first extra Estate on turn 18.1. Margrave’s spare buy follows the same thresholds rather than a flat Estate rule: with $5 or more left over it takes a Duchy once four Provinces are gone, with $3 or $4 a Silver, and an Estate only once the Province pile is down to its last one. A $10 turn is therefore a Province and nothing else. Buying Estates alongside Provinces earlier is a Groundskeeper plan, and this deck owns no Groundskeeper.

Playing the deck

Villages first, then Junk Dealer while it still has junk to eat, then the draw terminals, then Monument. Once only three Coppers are left Junk Dealer prefers an Estate to a Copper — Estates gained under Groundskeeper have already scored their token, so eating them afterwards is free. That three-Copper mark is a preference between junk, not a reserve: Junk Dealer's trash is mandatory, so a hand holding no junk at all still gives up its cheapest spare, and that is usually one of the last Coppers. The deck genuinely does end on one or two of them. Making the floor a hard reserve was measured and costs win rate, because the only thing left to feed Junk Dealer is then a Silver, which is worth more than the Copper it saves.

Library is the reason the villages come first. It draws up to seven cards and sets aside Actions you cannot play, so a hand that has already banked two or three +Actions turns Library into a genuine refill rather than a hand of stranded terminals. With no Actions left the strategy sets aside every Action, villages included: Library puts a kept card in hand without playing it, and nothing on this board grants Villagers, so a village drawn at zero Actions is dead weight that costs a replacement draw. Setting villages aside as well was worth 1.3 points of win rate over keeping them.

Margrave's attack matters less than its buy. Against these opponents the discard-to-three does real work in the greening turns, but the deck is built around the +1 Buy that lets a $13 turn become a Province and a Duchy, or an $11 turn a Province and a Silver.

Why Groundskeeper loses here

Groundskeeper is the most exciting card on the board and the search ranked it near the bottom. Of the fifteen best candidates in the broad screen, fourteen bought zero Groundskeepers. No Groundskeeper policy reached the finalist round robin on merit — the only entrant that bought any was the seeded panel engine, and it placed eleventh of twelve. The recommended deck beat it in 284 of 300 validation games.

The reason is buys. Groundskeeper only outscores Monument when the turn gains at least two victory cards, and nothing on this board supplies extra buys except a single Margrave. A deck with one spare buy gains two green cards on a good turn, which is two tokens from a played Groundskeeper — but Monument delivers one token and $2 towards the Province itself, and it does so on every turn from the moment it is bought rather than only during greening.

The board contains no Market, no Festival, no Bridge, and no way to gain victory cards other than buying them. Groundskeeper wants a kingdom this one is not.

This is a result about these implemented policies on this fixed two-player kingdom, not a general claim about Groundskeeper. A version of this board with a $3 Buy-granting card would very likely reverse it.

The three closest results are the honest ones. The runner-up, the third-place engine and the Monument-free version of this engine lose at 64.0%, 59.7% and 58.3%, which is a real but modest edge; the recommendation is the best policy found, not a proven optimum. Dropping Monument from the same engine is worth 12.2 points of round-robin win rate and 3.4 points of mean final score head to head, which isolates the payload's contribution directly.

What the winning deck looks like

Averaged over the validation games, the recommended deck finished with about four Fishing Villages, 1.6 Border Villages, 1.9 Libraries, one Margrave, two Monuments, 1.9 Junk Dealers, 1.5 Silvers and 0.7 Gold, plus 4.1 Provinces, 1.5 Duchies and 0.8 Estates. It had trashed its starting Coppers from seven down to 1.2 and its Estates from three down to 0.8. Games ran about 18 to 21 turns.

How the board was chosen

At the time of the draw the catalog held 134 registered strategies and 410 kingdom cards with no explicit strategy-priority or Way-rule reference, excluding basic and starting cards. The ten piles were selected from that pool by hand to give a complete cost curve and two genuinely competing payloads, then fixed before any strategy for the board was written.

“Unused” means no explicit references in the catalog before this work. It does not establish that a card was absent from shared tactical defaults, automatic gains, or written guides. Registering the strategy on this page necessarily makes six of the ten piles used; the card usage report shows the current state.

Simulator work done for this board

Three per-card decisions on this board had a hook on the base AI that GeneticAI never forwarded to the strategy, so no strategy could override them. All three are now forwarded, with tests: should_trash_copper_for_moneylender, should_keep_library_action, and choose_card_to_trash_with_junk_dealer. The last of these is listed as “needs forwarding” in the repository's shared card decisions inventory. Strategies that define no hook keep the previous defaults unchanged.

Reproduce the experiments

Run these from the repository root. The search script records full parameters and a per-match seed, and every shuffle seed is played from both starting seats.

PYTHONPATH=. python scripts/search_groundskeeper_margrave.py search \
    --games 24 --output .context/groundskeeper-search.json

PYTHONPATH=. python scripts/search_groundskeeper_margrave.py tournament \
    --input .context/groundskeeper-search.json \
    --games 100 --finalists 8 --output .context/groundskeeper-tournament.json

PYTHONPATH=. python scripts/search_groundskeeper_margrave.py validate \
    --games 300 --output .context/groundskeeper-validation.json

To play the published strategy against any other registered entrant on this board:

python compare_all_strategies.py --board boards/groundskeeper_margrave.txt --games 40

That command currently finds only two eligible entrants. Because every pile here was unused, every other registered strategy references a card this board does not have, and the tool excludes them. The one pairing it can run is against Big Money, which the published strategy won 39–1. The round robin above is the substantive comparison; this is the catalog-level check.
